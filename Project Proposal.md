  In this project, we compare running data collected from a professional soccer team with
data from the Vassar College Men’s Soccer team. We will be doing this through answering 4
main research questions. 1. How is sprint data correlated with match outcome and is there a
significant difference between amateur and professional football? 2. How does game intensity,
based on distance and sprint information, change based on the competition? 3. How can the data
be used to make predictions about player performance in upcoming competitions? 4. Are subs
undervalued in professional culture as we compare sprint data among starting players and
substitute players at the division 3 level (social element)? As we answer these research questions
we will go more in depth into sprint metrics such as looking at accelerations, decelerations, and
distance covered under high metabolic load (HMLD) to see if they tell a different story from
high speed running (HSR)/sprinting (SPR)? Based on our research questions, some details we
can expect to find are a weak to medium correlation between running and winning, also a weak
to medium correlation between game intensity and competition type, total distance and top speed
being more easily predicted than complicated metrics such sprint distance, and that substitute
players work at a higher intensity per minute compared to starters.
  Our motivation for these research questions are driven by our involvement on the men’s
soccer team and our love for the sport. We watch and play soccer every single day but have not
stopped to ask ourselves key questions about our performance and what it means for us as
individuals and teams. Furthermore, we have access to data similar to the dataset that we will be
utilizing through our tracking technology, Beyond Pulse, that we wear every day during our
practices and games. This technology is able to track similar statistics as the data set, like
distance covered, number of sprints, workload, and heart rate data. While there are various
differences in the personal data that we have and the dataset that we are working with, there is
some overlap. One of the catalysts for our research question and exploration of the dataset is the
possibility to use our findings in our own environment. As a team, we feel that we do not use the
data to its full potential, and we are leaving lots of gains on the table. The exploration of the
dataset in the professional setting can help create a blueprint for future use within our program to
better understand the work that is being done on the field. Additionally, our exploration into how
sprint data is correlated with match outcome will be quite interesting to explore and could even
shift the way that we approach games this season. All in all, this topic is a bridge between our
love for the sport and the skills that we have learned in the classroom.
  The two datasets that we will be working with are Running Match Performance in Elite
Soccer Player - full season dataset and Beyond Pulse data collected on the Vassar Men’s Soccer
team. The first dataset consists of running match performance data of professional male soccer
players competing in domestic and international competitions. Each row is an anonymous player
identified only by position and with a placeholder number assignment. The data is organized in
chunks, with each chunk representing an individual match. General information about the
matches and players like location (Home/Away), result, minutes played, competition, and date.
The rest of the data is quantitative and focuses solely on the physical performance of the athletes.
Columns provide data on total distance covered, distance covered in high metabolic load, relative
rate of distance covered, number of high-intensity accelerations and decelerations, top speed,
distance covered at high speeds, sprinting distance, total distance covered at high-speeds and
sprinting, and entries into high-speed and sprint zones. The data has 584 rows. Data appears to
only represent players who played 75 minutes or more, offering a possible bias that could skew
the data. This results in a cast of characters who float in and out of the data - not every player is
present in every match, so we only have access to tracking data that represents the physical load
produced by equal to or greater than 75 minutes of game time. For our second dataset, although
we currently do not have access to it at this moment we can expect to see data on player,
position, minutes played, ethnicity, hometown, heart rate zones (uses percentages of max heart
rate), active participation percentages, workload (calculated through the metrics total distance
covered, sprint distance, number of sprints/acel/decel), total distance covered. **We have talked
to our assistant coach and he most likely will be able to compile this dataset for us. If not we will
reach out to the Beyond Pulse team to get the dataset**
  In order to analyze the datasets we will first clean the professional stats and reformat it so
that it the columns are sorted by the individual players. From there we will compare various
statistics against each other and attempt to find correlations that answer our research questions.
Then, we will format and clean the Vassar Men’s Soccer to match the professional player dataset
as close as possible. Some potential ways we will try to find correlations are by using different
types of visuals, linear regression, and other data techniques that we learn throughout the class.
